## Short answer

**No — gross margin is not a relevant metric for JPMorgan Chase, and it cannot be meaningfully computed from its financials.** JPM is a bank (a financial holding company), not a company that produces and sells goods. The terms "gross margin" and "cost of goods sold" do not appear anywhere in the entire 382-page 2022 Form 10-K — the filing simply has no such concept in it.

## Why gross margin doesn't apply to a bank

Gross margin = (Revenue − Cost of goods sold) / Revenue presupposes a business that buys or makes things and sells them. JPM earns revenue by **intermediating money** — lending, deposits, trading, investment banking, asset management — and its income statement is structured completely differently (10-K, Consolidated statements of income, p. 159):

| Income statement section ($M) | 2022 | 2021 | 2020 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Noninterest revenue (fees, IB, markets, asset mgmt) | 61,985 | 69,338 | 65,388 |
| Interest income | 92,807 | 57,864 | 64,523 |
| Interest expense | (26,097) | (5,553) | (9,960) |
| **Net interest income** | 66,710 | 52,311 | 54,563 |
| **Total net revenue** | 128,695 | 121,649 | 119,951 |
| Provision for credit losses | 6,389 | (9,256) | 17,480 |
| Total noninterest expense | 76,140 | 71,343 | 66,656 |
| Pre-tax income | 46,166 | 59,562 | 35,815 |

The structural reasons:

1. **The COGS-like cost is already netted inside revenue.** The closest thing JPM has to a "cost of goods" is interest expense — the cost of the raw material (funding) that the bank lends out. But banks present it netted against interest income *within* the top line (net interest income). There is no "gross revenue" line from which to net costs, so a gross margin has no numerator or denominator.
2. **Operating costs aren't production costs.** Noninterest expense is mostly compensation ($41.6B of $76.1B in 2022 — ~55%), technology, occupancy, and professional fees — semi-fixed operating overhead, not variable cost that scales with each unit of "goods" sold.
3. **The cost of the lending business is the provision for credit losses**, which is a reserve-based (CECL), inherently cyclical estimate — not a cost of goods sold.

To illustrate how meaningless a forced calculation is: if you treated interest expense as "COGS," JPM's pseudo-gross-margin would be ~92.3% (2020), ~95.6% (2021), and ~83.1% (2022) — swinging 3–13 points a year purely because interest expense rose ~4.7× (from $5.6B to $26.1B) as rates went from zero. The metric would measure the Fed, not JPM's operating economics.

## Bottom line

Gross margin isn't a relevant or computable metric for JPM, so the question isn't answerable on those terms — and any forced construction of one is meaningless. If you translate the *spirit* of the question ("are its margins historically consistent within ~2 points a year?") onto the metrics banks actually use: JPM's core spread metric (NIM) is indeed quite stable (moves of ≤0.4 pts/year), its overhead ratio is roughly stable (one ~3-point move in 2021 when fee revenue spiked), and its bottom-line profitability margins (pre-tax margin, ROE/ROA) are **not** stable — they swing well beyond 2 points a year with the credit cycle (provision swings from +$17.5B in 2020 to −$9.3B in 2021 to +$6.4B in 2022) and interest rates. That volatility is normal and expected for a bank; margin stability in the industrial/goods-company sense is simply not a meaningful quality signal for JPMorgan.
