## Short answer

Gross margin is **not a relevant metric for JPMorgan Chase (JPM)**, so the question of whether it is "historically consistent (within ~2% per year)" cannot meaningfully be answered. JPM is a universal bank / financial holding company, and its financial statements contain no gross-margin concept at all.

## Why gross margin doesn't apply

- **No cost of goods sold, no gross profit line.** JPM's Consolidated statements of income (2022 Form 10-K, p. 159) go straight from revenue lines to operating results with no COGS or gross-profit subtotal. The full expense structure is: provision for credit losses, noninterest expense (compensation, occupancy, technology, professional services, marketing, other), and income tax — none of these is a "cost of revenue" tied to units of product sold.
- **Revenue itself is already partly netted.** "Total net revenue" = noninterest revenue (fees, principal transactions, asset management, card income, etc.) **plus net interest income** (interest income minus interest expense). A bank's single largest "input cost" — interest paid on deposits and borrowings — is embedded *inside* the revenue figure, not below it. There is no clean gross level to compute a margin from.
- **The business is not selling goods.** Gross margin measures price over production cost of physical/products. JPM earns spread income on a ~$3.3 trillion balance sheet and fee income on services; its "margins" are spreads and ratios, not unit economics. The word "gross" appears in the entire 10-K only in unrelated contexts (gross charge-offs, gross derivative receivables/payables, gross GDP, gross IB revenue for revenue-sharing).
- **Any "gross margin" you see for JPM in a screener is an artifact** of the data vendor mapping some cost line (or nothing) to COGS — it is not a reported or comparable figure, and its level is arbitrary.

## The margin metrics banks actually use (from this 10-K, FY2020–FY2022)

| Bank metric | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | Year-over-year move |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Net yield on interest-earning assets (NIM, FTE) | 1.98% | 1.64% | 2.00% | −0.34 pp, then +0.36 pp |
| Overhead ratio (efficiency ratio) | 56% | 59% | 59% | +3 pp, then 0 pp |
| Pre-provision profit ÷ total net revenue (computed) | 44.4% | 41.4% | 40.8% | −3.0 pp, then −0.6 pp |
| Return on assets | 0.91% | 1.30% | 0.98% | — |

So, if you map the spirit of your question onto the closest analogue, **net interest margin was quite stable** (moves of ~0.35 pp, well inside a 2 pp band, driven by the rate cycle: down when rates were at zero, up 36 bps in 2022). The **overhead ratio** stepped 3 pp in 2021 — slightly beyond a "roughly 2%" swing — but that reflects revenue mix (the 2021 revenue surge and 2022 expense growth), not a gross-margin concept. Neither is a gross margin, and neither behaves like one.

Note on "historically": the attached 10-K provides only three years of income-statement history (2020–2022); a longer gross-margin history does not exist in any case, and longer NIM/overhead history would require prior-year filings.
